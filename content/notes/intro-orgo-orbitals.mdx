However, if you are a pre-med student trying to pass organic chemistry, I doubt you will care, and you are the target audience. Here, I will simplify atomic and molecular orbitals to an <Hl>extremely basic level</Hl>.

This explanation may make chemistry and physics majors unhappy. So this theory will be called <O>Intro Orgo Orbital Theory</O>, which will model actual orbital theory.

## the basics

<Margin>"orbital" just means<br/>"where the electron<br/>probably is"</Margin>

Orbitals are places where electrons live. Electrons come in two phases, <Hl>in phase</Hl> and <Hl tone="pink">out of phase</Hl>, and opposite phases don't like each other.

Orbitals on neighbouring atoms interact: that's a **bond**.

- single bond = σ
- double bond = σ + π
- triple bond = σ + π + π

---

## rule 1: pi electrons live in p orbitals

A p orbital is an hourglass: <U>one orbital, two lobes</U>, opposite phases, up to two electrons.

<POrbital />

Two p orbitals side by side, same phase: a <Hl>π bond</Hl>.

<PiBond />

## rule 2: a row of p orbitals is one pi system

<Conjugation />

An sp³ carbon has no p orbital, so <Hl tone="pink">the row stops there</Hl>. More room to spread out = more stable.

## rule 3: hybridization is what's left over

<Hybridization />

1. sp³: <O>zero</O> p orbitals left
2. sp²: <O>one</O>, so one π bond
3. sp: <O>two</O>, so two π bonds

## rule 4: hybridization sets the shape

<Shapes />

<Margin>spin methane. it is<br/>not the flat cross<br/>you draw on paper</Margin>

A π bond needs parallel p orbitals. Twist it and <U>the overlap disappears</U>:

<Twist />

Not <X>"double bonds can't rotate"</X>: rotating one breaks the π bond.

### a confusing example: CH₂=C=CH₂

The middle carbon makes two π bonds, so it needs two p orbitals: it's <O>sp</O>, not <X>sp² "because it's a double bond"</X>. Its two p orbitals are at 90°, so the two ends are too.

<Allene />

<X>"double bonds make a molecule flat"</X> → each π bond is flat on its own.

## rule 5: more s character, electrons held closer

sp³ 25% s · sp² 33% · sp 50%. <Hl tone="green">Closer to the nucleus = a happier negative charge.</Hl>

<SCharacter />

<Sticky front="exam answer, copy this">"The conjugate base puts the negative charge in an orbital with <em>more s character</em>, which holds the electrons closer to the nucleus and stabilizes the conjugate base."</Sticky>

## rule 6: bad angles bend bonds

Cyclopropane's ring is 60°; carbon wants 109.5°. The bonds bend outward: <O>banana bonds</O> ([bent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bent_bond)).

<BentBonds />

## rule 7: two orbitals in, two orbitals out

Matching phases → <Hl>bonding</Hl> (lower). Opposite phases → <Hl tone="pink">antibonding</Hl> (higher, with a node).

<MODiagram />

## rule 8: antibonding orbitals have stars

σ → σ\*, π → π\*. The star is a <O>warning sign</O>: electrons in it weaken that bond.

## rule 9: a row of p orbitals makes a ladder

This is the diagram your textbook draws for butadiene. It's rule 7 again, with more orbitals: <U>n p orbitals in, n π orbitals out</U>.

<PiLadder />

1. each rung up adds one <O>node</O> (a phase flip, red dashes)
2. electrons fill from the bottom, two per rung
3. the top full rung is the <Hl>HOMO</Hl>, the first empty one is the <Hl tone="pink">LUMO</Hl>

<Pen>it runs off fig. 3: change the molecule there and this redraws.</Pen>

## rule 10: HOMO = where the electrons come from

The highest full orbital: lone pairs, negative charges, π bonds.

## rule 11: LUMO = where the electrons go

The lowest empty orbital: empty p orbitals, σ\*, π\*.

<Hl>ELECTRONS IN THE HOMO ENTER THE LUMO.</Hl> That's most curved arrows.

## rule 12: nucleophile = HOMO, electrophile = LUMO

### example: carbocations

<Carbocation />

<Hl>A carbocation is an empty p orbital waiting for electrons.</Hl>

### example: SN2

<SN2 />

<U>Nucleophile HOMO → backside of σ\*<sub>C–LG</sub></U>. That's why it's <O>backside attack</O>.

### example: carbonyl attack

<Carbonyl />

<Margin>the ~107° has a name: the<br/><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BCrgi%E2%80%93Dunitz_angle">Bürgi–Dunitz angle</a>.<br/>not on your exam.</Margin>

Two arrows, one event: <Hl>electrons into π\*<sub>C=O</sub></Hl>.

## rule 13: pi bonds are common HOMOs

An arrow starting at a double bond = <U>π electrons from a HOMO into someone's LUMO</U>.

## rule 14: lone pairs join pi systems from p orbitals

<Amide />

Lone pair next to a π bond? If it can sit in a p orbital, <Hl>resonance</Hl>.

## rule 15: resonance is a bigger house

<Allyl />

Resonance structures are not <X>molecules flipping back and forth</X>. They're drawings of one real molecule.

## the short version

```
orbitals        = places electrons live
pi electrons    → live in p orbitals
p, p, p in a row → conjugation → resonance

sp3  →  0 p left   109.5°   25% s
sp2  →  1 p left   120°     33% s
sp   →  2 p left   180°     50% s

more s character  → anion closer to nucleus → more stable
n orbitals in     → n out: bonding below, antibonding (*) above
each rung up      → one more node
electrons into *  → that bond gets weaker

HOMO (nucleophile) ──electrons──▶ LUMO (electrophile)
```
